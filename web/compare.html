Drives from the Atlas survey

The survey stores each sighting once and keeps a record of every exact duplicate it set aside, so they are put back here and the duplicate figures match the logs. It holds Wi-Fi sightings that have a position: Bluetooth and the data-quality counts still need the log files themselves. Imports made before the survey kept those records come without their duplicates.

Comparisons saved in the Atlas survey

A saved comparison is a snapshot of the results on this page, with its settings, drive names and notes. It is kept in the survey's database (table compare_runs), so it travels with snapshots and can be read in the SQL browser. Opening one shows the stored results; to change settings, add the logs again.

Scheduler: channels, revisits and misses

What the logs say about where the scan time went and what it bought. Channel yield is the per-channel prior the scheduler's dwell table encodes; revisit interval is the sweep cycle as the APs saw it; misses by band and channel show what one rig's scan list left for the other to find.

Channel yield

Sightings per AP is how often the radio re-logged a network it already had on that channel: high means dwell it could spare, low means it barely caught them. The last four columns use the shared road with the baseline, so the baseline row leaves them blank.

Misses by band, on shared road

Of the APs one drive heard on the shared road, how many did the other hear too, by band.

Revisit interval and first sighting

Revisit is the gap between two sightings of the same network inside one encounter (60 s or less). On a multi-node rig the overall figure mixes every node; the per-node figure in Nodes is each node's own sweep. First signal is how loud a network was when it was first logged on the drive: a lower (more negative) figure means it was caught earlier on the approach. Loudest at first sight is the share of networks whose first sighting was also their strongest, so the approach was missed entirely.

AP history

Drives in time order, using every road driven. Moved means an AP's estimated position shifted more than 500 m since its last drive, which often points to a mobile hotspot.

Data quality

How the numbers work

  • Splits and merges: these change how rows are grouped and never edit your files. Saved CSVs keep each original row as written, including rows the analysis leaves out, except rows with an unreadable timestamp.
  • Networks per second: different networks logged in each second, added up, then divided by active time.
  • Duplicates per second: extra rows for a network already logged in that same second. On multi-node rigs this includes two nodes seeing the same AP. Exact duplicates also match on signal, channel and node.
  • New networks per second: first sighting of a network on that drive.
  • Time visible: from first to last sighting of a network during one encounter. An encounter ends after 60 seconds unseen. A network seen once counts as 0 seconds, because the log can't show how long it was in range.
  • Speed: measured from GPS over a few seconds around each row. An encounter's speed is the average at its first and last sighting.
  • Estimated APs: a capture-recapture estimate from how many APs two drives share. Weak APs are harder to catch, so it tends to run low.
  • AP position: a signal-weighted average of where the network was sighted. It's an estimate from driving past, and doesn't locate the AP exactly.
  • 95% range and p: the shared road is cut into 400 m stretches, one paired count of distinct APs per stretch (per pair of legs when two legs share 2 km or more). The range is the 2.5th to 97.5th percentile of the baseline-vs-it gap over 1,000 resamples of those stretches; p is a two-sided sign test on how many stretches each side won.
  • Fairness checks: shared road (2 km and 30% of each drive), speed mix (the two drives' time per speed band on shared road, 70% alike), direction (heading agrees on half the stretches), time of day (3 h, same weekday or weekend), GPS (no more than 10% of rows without a fix) and randomized MACs (no more than half of the networks, and the two drives within 15 points of each other). Thresholds are rules of thumb.
  • Channel yield: each row's Channel column. New APs are first sightings on the drive, the same thing the scheduler's yield EWMA counts. Share of sightings against share of APs shows where the radio's time went against what it found.
  • Revisit interval: seconds between consecutive sightings of one network within an encounter. Node overlap is the share of networks heard by two or more nodes within the same second, the mesh's own [PAIR] redundancy metric.
  • Devices: each network or Bluetooth device is identified from its MAC prefix, its name and its Bluetooth manufacturer id (the MfgrId column, when the logger writes it). Notable kinds come from the detection tables of Warble 3.1.321 (MAC prefixes for cameras, police and fleet gear, drones and tools; names for Flock, Flipper Zero, Pineapple and the like) and the buttplug.io list of adult toys' Bluetooth names. Everyday kinds come from names and from what the manufacturer usually makes. A randomized MAC has no manufacturer. A name can be faked or reused, so a match means "looks like", not proof. This needs the Signal Atlas app.
  • Street map: tiles load from OpenStreetMap, so its servers see which area the map shows. Your logs are never sent anywhere.
